# Root cause and fatigue-life improvement of welded conveyor frames in cold stores

## Verified facts
- AutomVoj reports that it does not currently have the equipment or know-how to test at low temperatures under cyclic load. [F1]
- Cold-store conveyors typically operate at 2–6 °C, with suspected thermal cycling, condensation and loaded-belt vibration; the company tests frames under load at room temperature. [F2]
- AutomVoj has photos and measurements of cracked joints from site visits and still has two failed sections in storage. [F3]
- The frames are mild-steel hollow sections, painted for corrosion resistance, MIG/MAG welded, with cracking reported at main crossmember-to-stringer connections. [F4]
- Erić's group routinely uses servo-hydraulic fatigue machines, a climate chamber to −60 °C and Abaqus FE models; the chamber and machine are shared, so extended campaigns need advance coordination. [F5]
- AutomVoj states it has no dedicated R&D department and that technical development happens informally through project work. [F6]
- Erić is open to contract research on fatigue and fracture testing of welded steel components and to joint projects involving low-temperature or combined loading fatigue testing. [F7]
- In Erić's low-temperature study, cruciform fillet-welded S355J2+N specimens tested at −40 °C showed a 22% decrease in fatigue ductility coefficient relative to room temperature. [F8]

## Our inference
- Erić's combination of low-temperature fatigue testing, residual-stress measurement and FE analysis appears well matched to a root-cause study, but his published low-temperature data are on other steels and specimen types, so transfer to AutomVoj's mild-steel hollow-section detail at 2–6 °C must be tested rather than assumed.
- The 22% fatigue-ductility drop at −40 °C is a possible cold-service risk, not evidence about AutomVoj's frames at 2–6 °C; the first experiment should treat it as a hypothesis and check the transfer gap.
- Erić's residual-stress and post-weld heat-treatment work suggests welding procedure, filler or joint-design changes may lower weld-toe crack driving force, but the benefit for this production detail is unproven.

## Proposed next steps
- Proposed first experiment: machine fatigue specimens from the two failed sections and from new production crossmember-to-stringer details in mild-steel hollow section, MIG/MAG welded and painted. Run a common mechanical load spectrum at room-temperature dry baseline, 2–6 °C isothermal, and 2–6 °C with slow thermal cycling; add condensation as a separate condition if humidity control is available, changing one suspected factor at a time. Measure cycles to 5% stiffness drop, crack initiation at the weld toe and post-test crack location. Company contribution proposed: supply failed and new sections, weld procedure, filler, paint and any site vibration or load records; researcher contribution proposed: specimen design, shared climate-chamber/servo-hydraulic testing and fracture analysis. Go/no-go: proceed to mechanism study only if a cold or thermal condition shows a statistically significant life reduction versus the room-temperature baseline; if not, stop the thermal hypothesis and test vibration or condensation separately. Proposed duration: six-month feasibility phase.
- If the first experiment passes, compare welding procedure, filler material and joint-design variants, including post-weld heat treatment where applicable, under the worst condition identified. Proposed success criterion: a measurable increase in cycles to crack initiation versus the current production detail, with the target agreed after baseline results.
- Use FE and residual-stress measurements to rank the likely crack drivers and draft a revised joint detail or welding change. Proposed deliverable: a cause ranking and draft procedure change reviewed with AutomVoj's chief engineer.
- Proposed timeline as negotiation target: months 1–2 specimen preparation and shared chamber scheduling; months 3–6 first experiment; months 7–9 variant comparison if go; month 10 review and draft procedure change.
