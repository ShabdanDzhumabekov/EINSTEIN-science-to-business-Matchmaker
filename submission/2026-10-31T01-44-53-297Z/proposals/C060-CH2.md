# Stop-level ETA prediction for PassengerView on a 340-bus AVL archive

## Verified facts
- The Romanian contracts require PassengerView ETAs within 90 seconds of actual arrival at least 85 percent of the time; the company says current timetable-plus-offset projection drifts badly in peak-hour traffic, roadworks or bad weather. [F1]
- Contracts have a twelve-month implementation window from go-live approval, expected Q3 2024. [F2]
- Work relies on full historical AVL data and timetable logs under a signed data-sharing agreement; the PostgreSQL time-series table has roughly 2.8 billion rows from 340 buses with ten-second pings and fields timestamp, vehicle ID, line ID, stop sequence, door-open events and odometer. [F3]
- The stack uses Java 17/Spring Boot, GTFS/GTFS-Realtime, and Docker/Kubernetes on an on-premise Subotica cluster; staffing is one senior backend engineer and one domain specialist part-time, one Python data engineer lacking time-series background, and no integrated weather data. [F4]
- Novaković's dissertation used Bayesian state-space models for bus travel time from archived AVL data; her work reports 18% segment-level MAE reduction and open-source preprocessing modules. Her workload is moderate; she can take one new collaboration in 2026 and prefers publishing anonymised results. [F5]
- Risk from her work: a Novi Sad model fine-tuned on Subotica reached 52 seconds MAE per stop-to-stop segment, but topology differences limited gains beyond about 8 weeks of fine-tuning; conformal prediction held 90% coverage at the cost of wider intervals. [F6]

## Our inference
- Her reported errors are segment-level or 15-minute-ahead, not the end-to-end 90-second/85% contract metric, so the SLA must be measured on company data.
- Her filter assumes 30–90-second pings while the company logs every ten seconds; denser data likely changes preprocessing assumptions and needs revalidation.
- Lightweight nightly-retrained models likely fit the company's limited Python maintenance capacity better than GPU-heavy models, suggesting state-space or fine-tuned GRU with a retraining runbook.

## Proposed next steps
- First experiment (proposed): use 12 months of the 340-bus AVL archive; baselines are today's timetable-plus-offset PassengerView and a historic-average segment model. Re-run her preprocessing on the 10-second stream; compare a revalidated state-space model and a company-fine-tuned GRU. Measure the contract metric—share of displayed stop-level ETAs within 90 seconds—on held-out weeks, stratified by peak, off-peak, roadworks and bad weather, with actual arrival as door-open event plus a stop-line sensitivity check. Proposed go/no-go: at least 85% within 90 seconds overall and no scope stratum below a negotiated floor; otherwise stop. Researcher contributes modelling and evaluation design; company contributes data access, timetable logs and acceptance conditions.
- Sign a data-sharing agreement and write a joint evaluation protocol defining actual arrival, scope, disruption strata, and the 90-second/85% computation. Proposed timeline as negotiation target: protocol month 1, experiment months 2–6, serving and handover months 7–12; success criteria are the SLA plus a reproducible retraining and monitoring runbook.
- Isolate one suspected drift factor at a time: compare roadworks-only and bad-weather-only cases with and without integrated weather, so results say which factor matters. Test door-open events as a dwell proxy where passenger counters are absent, and report where load-aware gains do not transfer.
- Plan serving and handover: expose the Python model behind a REST service consumed by the Java/Spring PassengerView path and GTFS-Realtime output on the Kubernetes cluster, with nightly retraining on a standard server and a data-engineer runbook.

## Open questions
- What exactly counts as actual arrival for the 90-second/85% SLA—door-open event, stop-line crossing or another signal—and which lines, periods and disruption conditions are in scope?
- Will the full historical AVL archive and timetable logs be available under a signed data-sharing agreement, and what known data-quality issues exist in the 2.8-billion-row table?
- Can weather data be integrated, or should the first model be weather-blind; and are passenger counters available, or only door-open events and odometer?
- The researcher's availability is not confirmed: can she confirm capacity and effort from 2026, and can the company accept her preference to publish anonymised results?
- How will the Python model be served and owned inside the Java/Spring Kubernetes stack after the project, and are Q3 2024 go-live, the 12-month window, and any budget or compute allocation still operative?
